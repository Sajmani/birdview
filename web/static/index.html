Where the numbers come from

Solid lines are measured. Dashed lines are estimated. So a bill and a tail are drawn solid, a neck and a body dashed, and the head is left hollow — a dash means nothing on a closed shape, so an empty outline says it instead.

The wing crossbar is split. Its solid outer ends are the measured wing chord, from the bend of the wing to the longest primary; the tick marks show where that measurement reaches. Only the span between the ticks is estimated, and it is drawn dashed when it is — so for two thirds of these birds the wingtips are real and the middle is inferred.

The cube is the bird's mass drawn as water: a cube of water weighing exactly what the bird does, at the same scale. Every cube stands on the floor of its drawing, so two can be compared directly — a Thick-billed Murre and a Scissor-tailed Flycatcher are nearly the same length, and the murre's cube dwarfs the flycatcher's. A cube root flattens, though: twice as wide is eight times as heavy, so the number under it is what states the mass.

A ~ after a measurement means the same thing in the numbers. Most lengths are estimated: no open dataset publishes body length for North American birds, so BirdView models it and says so.

Click any bird for every measurement, including each part of the figure, with the source or the model behind it named individually.

Controlsorder, scale, units and how birds are named
Order by
Sort every bird by total length, wingspan or mass, and group them into sections of similar size. Frequency orders by how much of what people record is that bird, most common first, and groups by powers of ten — one tier for the birds that are one record in a thousand, the next for one in ten thousand, and so on. Those tier edges do not move when you change the region filter, so the same tier means the same thing on every view.
Scale
Per section rescales each section to itself, so every bird is big enough to read. This is the default. Global draws everything shown at one scale instead — a hummingbird really is a speck beside a swan. Global is the honest view across families; per section is the better one for comparing shape within a group. Under Frequency the default is Global, because a frequency tier holds birds of every size and rescaling each tier to its own largest bird would draw a 140 mm bird the height of a swan.
Section step
How much larger a bird must be to start a new section. Smaller values give more, tighter sections. Under Frequency it sets how wide a tier is instead: 10× gives a tier per power of ten, 100× gives half as many, twice as wide.
Units
Inches and ounces, or centimeters and grams.
Names
Which English name to use. N. America is what birders here say and what eBird shows in the US and Canada; World is the international name, which the IOC and AviList use. They differ for 82 of these birds — the Dovekie is a Little Auk everywhere else. The scientific name, the code in brackets and the address of the page never change, so a link still means the same bird whichever you pick.
Compare birds
Turns on picking: every bird gets a + Pick button, and a bar along the bottom collects what you have picked. Compare then draws them side by side at one shared scale, so their sizes can be read directly against one another — which the list can only do for birds that land in the same tier. Filtering still works while you pick, so you can narrow to one family and choose from it. Click Compare birds again, or Clear in the bar, to put it away.

Regions and monthsnarrow to states, provinces, stretches of open sea, or times of year
Month
The whole year is selected when you arrive. Click a month to drop it, or All to clear the lot — so one month is two clicks. Months narrow the birds shown, not just the numbers: pick June and you get the birds that are here in June, counted against June's birdwatching rather than the year's. A bird BirdView has too few records of is shown in every month and says so, because its records show when somebody visited rather than when the bird was there.
Region
Everywhere is selected when you arrive. Click a state or province to drop it, a zone to drop the whole group, or All to clear the lot and start picking. A bird is listed in a region when it is reported there often enough to be worth looking for, from eBird records — and from iNaturalist where eBird withholds a sensitive species.
Zones
The same regions, grouped two ways; pick from either. Common birds is measured — regions are clustered by how much their species lists actually overlap, which puts Arizona with New Mexico and Texas. Geographic is the usual map, drawn by hand, so that someone looking for Alaska finds it under Alaska; the measured grouping files Alaska with British Columbia. Both cross the border on purpose. A few places join nothing: Hawaii is too unlike the mainland, and Navassa shares no species at all with the other islands.
Pelagic
Birds of the open sea, which belong to no state at all — a Bermuda Petrel has hundreds of records off the coast and none on land. They are grouped by ocean, and the boundaries between those are ours and approximate. Seabirds that also come within sight of shore, like the Sooty Shearwater, appear both here and in the states they are seen from.

Diet and Habitatnarrow by what a bird eats and where it lives
Diet
What a bird mostly eats, from AVONET, whose definition is quoted beside each option. Selecting two options shows birds in either, as the region and family filters do.
Habitat
Where a bird mostly lives, also from AVONET. Both are assigned per species, not per family: a Double-crested Cormorant and a Neotropic Cormorant need not share a habitat, and an Anhinga is a bird of swamps whatever its relatives do. Counts are of all 839 birds, not of what is currently shown.
One value from AVONET, more from us
AVONET records the one habitat and the one diet a species mainly uses, so a bird that lives in two places or eats two things is filed under one. BirdView adds whatever further habitats and diets apply — a Great Blue Heron on a tidal flat, a gull at a landfill — and tags each birdview rather than AVONET’s. The detail page for every bird lists each category with who assigned it, and everything BirdView adds is listed in one place.
