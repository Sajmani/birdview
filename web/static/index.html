BirdView

BirdView helps you understand the relative size of birds across families by providing abstract diagrams based on public and modeled data. Read Presentation for details, and use the Controls to configure the view. Contact birdview at ajmani dot net with feedback.

Where the numbers come from

Solid lines are measured. Dashed lines are estimated. So a bill and a tail are drawn solid, a neck and a body dashed, and the head is left hollow — a dash means nothing on a closed shape, so an empty outline says it instead.

The wing crossbar is split. Its solid outer ends are the measured wing chord, from the bend of the wing to the longest primary; the tick marks show where that measurement reaches. Only the span between the ticks is estimated, and it is drawn dashed when it is — so for two thirds of these birds the wingtips are real and the middle is inferred.

The cube is the bird's mass drawn as water: a cube of water weighing exactly what the bird does, at the same scale. Every cube stands on the floor of its diagram, so two can be compared directly — a Thick-billed Murre and a Scissor-tailed Flycatcher are nearly the same length, and the murre's cube dwarfs the flycatcher's. A cube root flattens, though: twice as wide is eight times as heavy, so the number under it is what states the mass.

A ~ after a measurement means the same thing in the numbers. Most lengths are estimated: no open dataset publishes body length for North American birds, so BirdView models it and says so.

Click any bird for every measurement, including each part of the diagram, with the source or the model behind it named individually.

Controls
Order by
Sort every bird by total length, wingspan or mass. Birds are then grouped into sections of similar size.
Scale
Per section rescales each section to itself, so every bird is big enough to read. This is the default. Global draws everything shown at one scale instead — a hummingbird really is a speck beside a swan. Global is the honest view across families; per section is the better one for comparing shape within a group.
Section step
How much larger a bird must be to start a new section. Smaller values give more, tighter sections.
Units
Inches and ounces, or centimeters and grams.
Names
Which English name to use. N. America is what birders here say and what eBird shows in the US and Canada; World is the international name, which the IOC and AviList use. They differ for 82 of these birds — the Dovekie is a Little Auk everywhere else. The scientific name, the code in brackets and the address of the page never change, so a link still means the same bird whichever you pick.
Compare birds
Turns on picking: every bird gets a + Pick button, and a bar along the bottom collects what you have picked. Compare then draws them side by side at one shared scale, so their sizes can be read directly against one another — which the list can only do for birds that land in the same tier. Filtering still works while you pick, so you can narrow to one family and choose from it. Click Compare birds again, or Clear in the bar, to put it away.

Regions
Region
Everywhere is selected when you arrive. Click a state or province to drop it, a zone to drop the whole group, or All to clear the lot and start picking. A bird is listed in a region when it is reported there often enough to be worth looking for, from eBird and iNaturalist records.
Zones
The same regions, grouped two ways; pick from either. Common birds is measured — regions are clustered by how much their species lists actually overlap, which puts Arizona with New Mexico and Texas. Geographic is the usual map, drawn by hand, so that someone looking for Alaska finds it under Alaska; the measured grouping files Alaska with British Columbia. Both cross the border on purpose. A few places join nothing: Hawaii is too unlike the mainland, and Navassa shares no species at all with the other islands.
Pelagic
Birds of the open sea, which belong to no state at all — a Bermuda Petrel has hundreds of records off the coast and none on land. They are grouped by ocean, and the boundaries between those are ours and approximate. Seabirds that also come within sight of shore, like the Sooty Shearwater, appear both here and in the states they are seen from.

Families
Family
Everything is selected when you arrive. Click a family to drop it, a group to drop the whole lot of them, or All to clear everything and start picking.
Groups
The same families, grouped two ways; pick from either. Birder groups are the names a field guide uses — Shorebirds, Birds of Prey, Owls & Nightbirds. They are drawn by hand and say how birders talk, not where a bird lives: “Shorebirds” means the sandpipers are a family birders call shorebirds, not that every sandpiper is on a beach. Taxonomic order is AviList's, in checklist sequence. The two disagree for most families, which is the interesting part — Birds of Prey draws from three orders, and the 43 families of Perching Birds scatter across a dozen birder groups.
